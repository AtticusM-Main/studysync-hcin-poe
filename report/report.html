1. Introduction

StudySync is a peer-to-peer website where university students form study groups, share resources and work on coursework together without the distractions of general social media. Part 1 analysed the users' needs and set out the design requirements. Part 2 turned those requirements into a working, coded prototype (HTML, CSS and JavaScript, no frameworks). This Portfolio of Evidence (POE) is the final version: it records the feedback received on Part 2, describes how the prototype was improved in response, and uses screenshots of the updated prototype to show how each HCI concept was implemented.

The prototype covers the four functional areas from Part 1: study group management (search, Quick Join, create, group workspace), real-time collaboration (chat, shared whiteboard, discussion board and, new in this version, shared notes), a resource repository (browse, filter, rate, report, upload) and progress monitoring (weekly goal, deadlines and, new in this version, a focus timer). It is a front-end prototype: data is simulated in the browser, while all interface behaviours (validation, feedback, undo, confirmations, keyboard support and saved preferences) work.

4. Usability goals

4.1 Effectiveness

Users can complete core tasks accurately: joining a group, uploading a resource and logging study hours. Forms use clear labels, hints and inline error messages, and results are confirmed on screen. A user who enters an impossible value (99 hours) is told exactly what is allowed instead of the form failing silently (Figure 4).

4.2 Efficiency

“Quick Join” on the dashboard and on every group card joins a group in one click, with immediate feedback (Figure 11). The dashboard lists recently visited groups so a workspace is one click away. The new focus timer removes a step from goal tracking: when a session ends, the session length is already entered in the “Log a study session” field, so the user only presses Log hours (Figure 6).

4.3 Safety

Destructive actions (deleting a post, clearing the whiteboard) open a confirmation dialog that names the consequence. After a post is deleted, a toast offers Undo. In the POE version, this toast stays for 12 seconds and does not disappear while the pointer or keyboard focus is on it, so slower users can still recover.

4.4 Utility

Every feature in the plan has a counterpart: workspace (chat, whiteboard, discussion, shared notes, calendar, members), resource library with upload, and personal progress tracking with a focus timer. Nothing was added that students do not need for collaborative study.

4.5 Learnability

The layout follows familiar conventions: top navigation, a dashboard, a primary “＋ Create” button, cards and tabs. First-time users get a three-step guide that links directly to each step (Figure 1). Icon buttons show tooltips on hover and focus, and form hints explain what to enter (Figure 9).

7. Types of interaction

  • Words – Action-oriented labels such as “Create Study Group”, “Share a resource”, “Log hours”, “Start” and “Send report” instead of generic “Submit”.
  • Visual representations – Recognisable icons for chat, whiteboard, discussion, notes and notifications; colour-coded chips (blue modules, green positive, amber deadlines); avatars show who is editing the notes.
  • Physical objects and space – A responsive grid (three columns on desktop, one on mobile) with a collapsing menu (Figure 15); touch-sized controls of at least 42 px.
  • Time – The focus timer counts down live and fills a progress bar; toasts appear and leave on their own; the notification badge pulses briefly. All motion is switched off for users who prefer reduced motion.
  • Behaviour – Typing a module code shows matching modules and filters groups instantly (Figure 13); whiteboard drawing follows the pointer (Figure 14); shared notes update when another member types (Figure 17).

9. Emotional interaction

Students are often stressed about deadlines and exams, so StudySync is designed to feel supportive rather than demanding. These choices draw on research linking emotional elements of a user interface to how users perceive and engage with it (Mori, Paternò and Furci, 2015; Owa, Suzuki and Yang, 2025).

  • Warm, personal greeting – “Good afternoon, Thandi!” with a study-streak message that recognises effort (Figure 1).
  • Positive reinforcement – “Welcome to … 🎉” after joining; “Every session counts – keep it up” after logging hours (Figure 20); a celebration when the weekly goal is reached.
  • Calm focus and wellbeing NEW – The timer opens with “Ready when you are. Phones down, notes out.”, encourages the user during the session, and ends with “Take a 5-minute break – stretch, drink water” (Figure 6), discouraging stressful cramming.
  • Reduced anxiety – Deadlines use calm amber chips with days left, not alarming red; errors are phrased kindly and never blame the user.
  • Belonging and recognition – Reputation points, a “Helpful Contributor” badge, and reactions such as 💡 Helpful and 🙏 Thanks that let peers show appreciation (Figure 16).

10. Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.0

The prototype was built to meet WCAG 2.0 Level AA (Caldwell et al., 2008) wherever it applies to its content. The table lists the criteria addressed under the four WCAG principles.

PrincipleCriterionImplementation
Perceivable1.1.1 Non-text contentDecorative icons are hidden from screen readers; the whiteboard and calendar have text alternatives; icon and reaction buttons have accessible names (e.g. “React Helpful, 1”).
1.3.1 Info and relationshipsSemantic landmarks, correct heading order, a label for every field (including the shared-notes area), and tab, list, timer and progressbar roles.
1.4.3 Contrast (minimum), 1.4.4 Resize textContrast was measured with the WCAG formula (see below): every text colour pair is at least 5.47:1, above the 4.5:1 minimum. A+ resizes text to 140% and the setting persists across pages; high-contrast mode is also remembered (Figures 3, 21).
Operable2.1.1 KeyboardEvery control works by keyboard: arrow keys on tabs and search suggestions, Enter to choose, Escape to close; timer, reactions and notes are native buttons and fields.
2.2.1 Timing adjustable NEWThe focus timer can be paused and reset. Toasts with an action (Undo) stay 12 seconds and pause while hovered or focused.
2.4.1 Bypass blocks, 2.4.7 Focus visibleA “Skip to main content” link appears on the first Tab (Figure 22); a 3 px dark-orange focus outline (#c2410c, 5.2:1 on white) marks every focusable element.
2.4.2 Page titled, 2.4.4 Link purposeEach page has a unique title (the timer adds the remaining time to it); links have meaningful text.
Understandable3.1.1 Language; 3.2.3/3.2.4 Consistencylang="en" on every page; header, navigation order, component styles and accessibility settings are identical on all pages.
3.3.1–3.3.3 Input assistanceRequired fields are marked; errors appear as text next to the field (role="alert") with a suggestion; hints explain the format.
Robust4.1.2 Name, role, valueCustom widgets expose ARIA states (aria-expanded, aria-pressed, aria-selected, aria-current) and live regions (role="status") for toasts, chat, autosave and result counts.

12. Final remarks

The Part 2 feedback confirmed that the prototype met its usability, UX, design-principle, interaction, social and emotional goals. For the POE, those strengths were deepened rather than redesigned: a focus timer supports the core study task, shared notes complete the collaboration requirement, reactions and onboarding make the experience friendlier and easier to learn, and accessibility moved from a general claim to measured, persistent behaviour. The most valuable next steps are usability testing with real students to measure task success and satisfaction, a screen-reader audit, and a back end so that chat, whiteboard and notes become truly real-time.
